# Region two proposal — Italia: the Rubicon crisis

**Episode and six-beat outline approved by the owner, 2026-10-25.** Region ID `italia`, episode **Caesar crosses the Rubicon: the opening of civil war**, **January 49 BC** in the contemporary Roman calendar, ending when Pompey/the consuls leave Rome. This initial proposal is retained as the research/decision record. The [full prose](italia-prose-review.md) and [artwork batch](italia-artwork-proposal.md) have separate pending approval gates; [current production status](italia-status.md) records the integrated temporary preview.

## Why this next

The PRD §9 launch list names Britannia before Italia but gives no mandatory production order or authored episode briefs. The inventory has an `italia` province and Coming soon route, with no campaign/questions. A second Roman region is the current priority ahead of M11; existing M0–M11 identifiers stay intact.

Italia offers a direct continuation from the accepted late-Republican Gallia setting: the same broad military period, an immediately legible boundary-crossing decision and a strong change of stakes from foreign siege to Roman civil conflict. That is a production recommendation, not a claim that one river alone caused the Republic's fall. It tests actual two-region discovery, quiz labels and progression without requiring new naval/chariot systems or a new province polygon. Britannia would need a substantial coastal/ship visual vocabulary; Dacia would require a different period/equipment/environment; Germania has no current province file. Those remain possible later content, not blocked engine features.

## Proposed six beats

This original outline specifies story function and sources. Full draft prose has now been written for separate approval; question writing/narration wait for that prose approval. January's fast-moving messages/occupations overlap; do not invent a precise daily chronology.

| Beat | Story function | Primary basis / treatment |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — An order from Rome | Senate pressure to relinquish command; negotiations narrow. | Caesar, *Civil War* I.1–6: explicitly his account of the political conflict. |
| 2 — The army he keeps | A veteran legion and Caesar's appeal to its loyalty. | Caesar I.7. His defence of the tribunes is a justification to examine, not a neutral verdict. |
| 3 — Across the boundary | Advance from Cisalpine Gaul into Italy; later writers dramatize the hesitation. | Plutarch, *Caesar* 32; Suetonius, *Julius* 31–33. Attribute the theatrical details. |
| 4 — Ariminum | Occupation makes the decision tangible. | Caesar I.8; compare Plutarch 32.8. Use an interpreted town approach, not later imperial monuments. |
| 5 — Terms and marching orders | Proposals and deployments proceed together. | Caesar I.9–13; compare contemporary correspondence before final wording. |
| 6 — Rome empties | Pompey and the consuls leave Rome; the war remains unresolved. | Cicero, *Atticus* VII.10–13; Rawson's synthesis. End here, without jumping to Caesar's later dictatorship. |

This episode requires neither a pitched battle nor a naval evacuation finale. March/idle/brace actions, restrained crowds, camera travel, sources and narration can carry the tension. Six beats and five questions fit the existing systems.

## References and disputed claims

Sources checked online on 2026-10-23:

- **Contemporary participant:** [Caesar, *Civil War* I.1–29](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Caesar/Civil_Wars/1A*.html), especially I.1–14. Use for the political framing, army and early advance; it is self-justifying and omits the named Rubicon set piece.
- **Contemporary counterweight:** [Cicero, *Letters to Atticus* VII](https://www.attalus.org/cicero/atticus7.html), especially 10–13 (E. O. Winstedt translation). Useful for uncertainty and political reaction; a partisan correspondent, not an omniscient military observer. The Perseus year-49 page was also located but could not be retrieved during this audit; it is not claimed read.
- **Later narrative traditions:** [Plutarch, *Life of Caesar* 32–33](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Caesar*.html#32) and [Suetonius, *Julius* 31–33](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/12Caesars/Julius*.html#31). The hesitation, speech, night journey and Suetonius' apparition are attributed literary accounts. No supernatural figure is proposed as a historical actor.
- **Modern historical control:** Elizabeth Rawson, [“Caesar: Civil War and Dictatorship,” *Cambridge Ancient History* IX, chapter 11](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-ancient-history/caesar-civil-war-and-dictatorship/C7F8E34AD8D803954A497D4E56707A53). The public chapter summary was inspected; full paid chapter access is not claimed. It explicitly cautions about the river's location, precise crossing date and the misleading seasonal inference from the contemporary calendar.
- **Modern source criticism:** Consuelo Martino, [“‘The Die Has Been Cast!’: Suetonius’ Caesar and the (G)looming Fate of Rome,” *Classical Quarterly*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/classical-quarterly/article/die-has-been-cast-suetonius-caesar-and-the-glooming-fate-of-rome/3FD7107D0DFF3667B3FC1AB56501EEF7), published online 27 July 2026, DOI 10.1017/S0009838826101438. Its comparison supports treating the famous scene as a later, shaped tradition rather than an eyewitness transcript.

Writing controls for the approved draft:

1. Use **January 49 BC**, not an unqualified exact January 10/11 date. The pre-Julian calendar was displaced from the seasons; do not add snow or “midwinter” as established fact.
2. Do not claim the modern river crossing point, bridge design or a plotted GPS route is settled. A small interpreted riverbank is enough.
3. Attribute the dice phrase and private hesitation to the later accounts; Caesar does not narrate that set piece himself. Sequence/location of the tribunes' arrival and the troop address also differ between accounts: avoid a false exact reconstruction.
4. Treat command legitimacy, personal motives and proposed peace terms as contested political claims. Avoid “the whole Senate wanted war” or “Caesar had no choice.”
5. Avoid precise troop totals when they add no teaching value; any used number must remain an attributed ancient claim. Crowd counts are composition, not an army census.
6. The crossing begins this civil-war episode; it does not instantly establish the Empire or complete Caesar's conquest of Italy. Independent history review remains a documented step before public launch.
